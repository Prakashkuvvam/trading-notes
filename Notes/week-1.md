# CHAPTER 1: WHAT IS A STOCK?

## The Basic Idea

Imagine you start a chai stall. You invest ₹50,000 of your own money. You buy equipment, raw materials, and start selling chai.

The business is 100% yours. You own it completely.

Now suppose the business grows. You want to open 5 more stalls, but you need ₹5,00,000. You don't have that money.

You have three choices:

1. **Take a loan from a bank** — You borrow money and pay it back with interest. The business is still 100% yours, but you have debt.

2. **Find a partner** — Someone gives you ₹5,00,000, and in return, they own a portion of your business. No debt, but you've given up some ownership.

3. **Sell small pieces of ownership to many people** — Instead of finding one partner, you divide your business into 10,000 small equal pieces and sell some of those pieces to many people.

**Each of those small pieces of ownership is called a SHARE or STOCK.**

---

## Formal Definition

A **stock** (also called a share or equity) represents partial ownership in a company.

If a company has issued 1,00,000 shares in total, and you own 1,000 shares, you own:

$$\frac{1,000}{1,00,000} \times 100 = 1\%$$

You own 1% of that company.

---

## Why Do Companies Issue Stocks?

Companies need money to:

- Expand operations
- Build new factories or offices
- Hire more employees
- Develop new products
- Pay off existing debt
- Fund research

Instead of borrowing all of this money (which creates debt and interest payments), they can sell ownership stakes (shares) to the public.

**The money raised by selling shares goes to the company.** In return, shareholders get:

- Partial ownership
- Right to receive dividends (if the company distributes profits)
- Right to vote on certain company decisions
- The ability to sell their shares to other people (this is what the stock market enables)

---

## Real Example: Reliance Industries

Reliance Industries Limited (RIL) has approximately **676 crore shares** outstanding (as of recent data).

If you buy 100 shares of Reliance, you own:

$$\frac{100}{6,76,00,00,000} \times 100 = 0.0000015\%$$

That's a tiny percentage, but it's still real ownership. You are legally a part-owner of Reliance Industries.

If Reliance declares a dividend of ₹10 per share, you receive:

$$100 \times ₹10 = ₹1,000$$

If Reliance's share price goes from ₹2,500 to ₹3,000, the value of your 100 shares increases from ₹2,50,000 to ₹3,00,000. You've made ₹50,000 in **capital appreciation** (on paper, until you sell).

---

## Key Terms to Remember

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Share / Stock** | A unit of ownership in a company |
| **Shareholder** | A person who owns shares |
| **Equity** | Another word for ownership / shares |
| **Dividend** | A portion of the company's profit distributed to shareholders |
| **Capital appreciation** | Increase in the value of your shares over time |
| **Outstanding shares** | Total number of shares that exist for a company |

# CHAPTER 2: PRIMARY MARKET vs SECONDARY MARKET

## How Shares Reach You

There are two stages in which shares are bought and sold.

### Stage 1: Primary Market (IPO)

When a company sells its shares to the public **for the first time**, it's called an **Initial Public Offering (IPO)**.

In the primary market:
- The company sells shares **directly to investors**
- The money goes **to the company**
- This happens only once per company (for each new offering)

**Example:**

In November 2021, Paytm (One97 Communications) had its IPO.
- The company offered shares at ₹2,150 per share
- Investors applied to buy these shares directly from the company
- Paytm raised approximately ₹18,300 crore from this IPO
- This money went to the company (and some existing shareholders who were selling their holdings)

### Stage 2: Secondary Market (Stock Exchange)

After the IPO, those shares start trading on a **stock exchange**. This is the secondary market.

In the secondary market:
- Investors buy and sell shares **among themselves**
- The company does NOT receive any money from these transactions
- The stock exchange facilitates this buying and selling
- **This is what most people refer to when they say "the stock market"**

**Example:**

After Paytm's IPO at ₹2,150, the shares listed on the stock exchange. On the first day, the price dropped to about ₹1,560. Since then, the price has fluctuated based on supply and demand among investors.

When you buy Paytm shares today on NSE, you're buying from another investor who is selling. Paytm (the company) gets nothing from this transaction.

---

## Visual Summary

```
COMPANY needs money
        ↓
    PRIMARY MARKET (IPO)
    Company sells shares to public
    Money goes TO the company
        ↓
    Shares are now LISTED on Stock Exchange
        ↓
    SECONDARY MARKET
    Investors buy and sell shares among themselves
    Money moves between INVESTORS, not to the company
```

# CHAPTER 3: STOCK EXCHANGES — NSE AND BSE

## What Is a Stock Exchange?

A stock exchange is a regulated marketplace where buyers and sellers of stocks come together to trade.

Think of it like an organized market. Just as vegetables are traded at a mandi (wholesale market), stocks are traded at a stock exchange.

Without a stock exchange:
- You'd have to personally find someone who wants to sell the exact stock you want to buy
- You'd have to negotiate the price one-on-one
- There would be no guarantee the other person would honor the deal
- There would be no transparency about prices

The stock exchange solves all of these problems by providing:
- A centralized platform for trading
- Real-time price discovery
- Guaranteed settlement (through clearing corporations)
- Rules and regulations

## Which One Matters More for You?

For **options and futures trading**, NSE is where virtually all the action happens.

Over 99% of F&O (Futures and Options) trading volume in India occurs on NSE.

When you eventually trade NIFTY options, you will be trading on NSE.

BSE has its own derivatives segment, but it has negligible volume compared to NSE.

**For this entire roadmap, we will focus on NSE.**

---

## How Do They Work?

Both exchanges are **fully electronic**. There is no physical trading floor anymore.

When you place an order through your broker's app (Zerodha, Groww, Dhan, etc.):

1. Your order goes to your broker's server
2. Your broker routes the order to the exchange (NSE or BSE)
3. The exchange's **matching engine** finds a corresponding buy/sell order
4. The trade is executed
5. The exchange confirms the trade to both parties
6. Settlement happens (money and shares are exchanged)

All of this happens in **milliseconds**.

---

## Important Concept: Listing

A company can be listed on one exchange or both.

Most large companies (Reliance, TCS, Infosys, HDFC Bank) are listed on **both NSE and BSE**.

Some smaller companies may be listed on only one.

The price of a stock can differ slightly between NSE and BSE at any given moment, but the difference is usually very small (a few paisa to a few rupees) and gets eliminated quickly by arbitrage traders.

# CHAPTER 4: WHAT IS NIFTY 50?

## The Problem Nifty Solves

There are approximately 2,200 companies listed on NSE. Their prices all move differently every day.

If someone asks you "How did the Indian stock market do today?", you can't describe the performance of all 2,200 companies.

You need a **summary number** — a single value that tells you how the overall market (or a representative portion of it) performed.

**That summary number is an INDEX.**

---

## What Is NIFTY 50?

NIFTY 50 is an **index** composed of **50 large and liquid companies** listed on NSE.

It is maintained by **NSE Indices Limited** (formerly India Index Services & Products Limited — IISL).

"NIFTY" comes from **N**ational Stock Exchange + F**IFTY** (50 companies).

---

## What Does the NIFTY 50 Number Mean?

When you see "NIFTY 50: 24,500" — this is not a price in rupees. It's an **index value** calculated using a formula.

The formula uses the **free-float market capitalization weighted method.**

Let me explain this step by step.

### Step 1: Market Capitalization

**Market capitalization** (market cap) = Share price × Total number of shares

**Example:**

| Company | Share Price | Total Shares | Market Cap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reliance | ₹2,500 | 676 crore | ₹16,90,000 crore |
| TCS | ₹3,800 | 366 crore | ₹13,90,800 crore |
| A small company | ₹50 | 10 crore | ₹500 crore |

### Step 2: Free-Float Market Capitalization

Not all shares of a company are available for public trading.

Some shares are held by:
- Promoters (company founders/owners)
- Government
- Strategic investors

These shares are not traded in the open market.

**Free-float shares** = Shares available for public trading

**Free-float market cap** = Share price × Free-float shares

**Example:**

Reliance has 676 crore total shares. Suppose promoters hold 50% (338 crore shares). The free-float shares are 338 crore.

Free-float market cap = ₹2,500 × 338 crore = ₹8,45,000 crore

### Step 3: How the Index Value Is Calculated

$$\text{NIFTY 50} = \frac{\text{Current total free-float market cap of all 50 companies}}{\text{Base market cap (from Nov 3, 1995)}} \times 1000$$

The base value was set at **1,000** on November 3, 1995.

So if the NIFTY is at 24,500, it means the free-float market cap of these 50 companies is **24.5 times** what it was on November 3, 1995.

**You don't need to memorize this formula.** What you need to understand is:

> NIFTY goes up when the share prices of its 50 component companies (especially the large-weight ones) go up.
> NIFTY goes down when those prices go down.

## Why the Weights Matter

Since NIFTY is **weighted by free-float market cap**, larger companies have a bigger impact on the index.

**Example:**

If HDFC Bank (weight ~13%) goes up 2%, it will push NIFTY up significantly.

If a smaller company in NIFTY (weight ~0.5%) goes up 10%, the impact on NIFTY will be minimal.

This means:

> **NIFTY's movement is dominated by its top 10–15 stocks.**

When you see NIFTY moving, your first question should be: "Which heavyweight stocks are driving this?"

You can check this in real-time on NSE or on TradingView.

# CHAPTER 8: WHAT ARE FUTURES AND OPTIONS? (Introduction Only)

**Note:** We will go deep into options in Phase 3 (Weeks 11–18). This is just an introduction so you understand the basic concept.

---

## What Is a Derivative?

A **derivative** is a financial instrument whose value is **derived from** another asset (called the **underlying asset**).

The underlying asset can be:
- A stock (e.g., Reliance shares)
- An index (e.g., NIFTY 50)
- A commodity (e.g., gold, crude oil)
- A currency (e.g., USD/INR)

**NIFTY options** are derivatives. Their underlying asset is the **NIFTY 50 index.**

---

## What Is a Futures Contract?

A **futures contract** is an agreement to buy or sell something at a predetermined price on a future date.

### Real-Life Analogy

You're a farmer. You grow wheat. It's currently March and your crop will be ready in June.

Current wheat price: ₹2,000 per quintal.

You're worried that by June, the price might drop to ₹1,500.

A buyer (say, a flour mill) is worried that by June, the price might rise to ₹2,500.

So you both agree today: **"In June, I will sell you 100 quintals of wheat at ₹2,100 per quintal, regardless of the market price at that time."**

This agreement is a **futures contract.**

| Scenario | Market Price in June | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| Price drops | ₹1,500 | Farmer is happy — he sells at ₹2,100 instead of ₹1,500 |
| Price rises | ₹2,500 | Buyer is happy — he buys at ₹2,100 instead of ₹2,500 |

**In a futures contract, both parties are OBLIGATED to honor the agreement.**

### NIFTY Futures Example

If NIFTY is at 24,500 and you buy a NIFTY futures contract at 24,520 (futures price is usually slightly different from spot):

- If NIFTY goes to 24,700 by expiry → You profit ₹180 per unit × 75 (lot size) = ₹13,500
- If NIFTY goes to 24,300 by expiry → You lose ₹220 per unit × 75 = ₹16,500

**Both profit and loss are unlimited.** This is why futures are risky.

---

## What Is an Options Contract?

An **options contract** gives the buyer the **RIGHT** (but not the obligation) to buy or sell something at a predetermined price before or on a specific date.

The buyer pays a **premium** for this right.

### Real-Life Analogy

You find a house you want to buy. Price: ₹50 lakhs.

You're not sure yet — you need to arrange financing, check documents, etc.

You pay the owner ₹50,000 and say: **"Give me 3 months. During these 3 months, I have the RIGHT to buy this house at ₹50 lakhs. If I decide not to buy, I lose the ₹50,000, but I don't have to buy."**

That ₹50,000 is the **PREMIUM.**

The ₹50 lakhs is the **STRIKE PRICE.**

The 3-month deadline is the **EXPIRY.**

| Scenario | What Happens |
|---|---|
| House price rises to ₹60 lakhs | You exercise your right, buy at ₹50 lakhs, and your net gain is ₹10 lakhs – ₹50,000 = ₹9,50,000 |
| House price drops to ₹40 lakhs | You don't buy. You lose only the ₹50,000 premium. |

**As a buyer, your maximum loss is limited to the premium paid.**

This is the fundamental difference between futures (unlimited loss) and options (limited loss for buyer).

---

## Two Types of Options

### 1. Call Option (CE — Call European)

A call option gives the buyer the right to **BUY** the underlying at the strike price.

**You buy a call when you think the price will GO UP.**

### 2. Put Option (PE — Put European)

A put option gives the buyer the right to **SELL** the underlying at the strike price.

**You buy a put when you think the price will GO DOWN.**

---

### NIFTY Options Example

**Scenario:** NIFTY is at 24,500. You think NIFTY will go up.

You buy: **NIFTY 24,500 CE (Call Option)** expiring this Thursday

Premium: ₹150 per unit

Lot size: 75 units (changed to 25 as per recent lot size updates — **always check the current lot size**)

**Let's calculate with lot size 75 for this example:**

Total premium paid = ₹150 × 75 = **₹11,250**

This ₹11,250 is the **maximum you can lose** on this trade.

| NIFTY at Expiry | Option Premium at Expiry | Your P&L per unit | Total P&L |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24,300 | ₹0 (expires worthless) | –₹150 | –₹11,250 |
| 24,400 | ₹0 (expires worthless) | –₹150 | –₹11,250 |
| 24,500 | ₹0 (ATM, no intrinsic value) | –₹150 | –₹11,250 |
| 24,600 | ₹100 | –₹50 | –₹3,750 |
| 24,650 | ₹150 | ₹0 | ₹0 (breakeven) |
| 24,700 | ₹200 | +₹50 | +₹3,750 |
| 24,800 | ₹300 | +₹150 | +₹11,250 |
| 25,000 | ₹500 | +₹350 | +₹26,250 |

**Notice:**
- Maximum loss = ₹11,250 (the premium you paid) — this happens if NIFTY stays at or below 24,500
- Breakeven = 24,500 + 150 = 24,650 (strike price + premium paid)
- Profit is theoretically unlimited as NIFTY goes higher

---

## Key Options Terms You Must Know

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Premium** | The price you pay (or receive) for an option | ₹150 per unit |
| **Strike price** | The price at which you can buy/sell the underlying | 24,500 |
| **Expiry** | The date on which the option contract expires | Every Thursday for NIFTY weekly options |
| **Lot size** | The fixed number of units in one options contract | 75 for NIFTY (verify current lot size) |
| **CE** | Call European — a call option | NIFTY 24,500 CE |
| **PE** | Put European — a put option | NIFTY 24,500 PE |
| **Option buyer** | Pays premium, has the RIGHT | Maximum loss = premium paid |
| **Option seller (writer)** | Receives premium, has the OBLIGATION | Maximum loss = potentially very large |

---

## ITM, ATM, OTM — Moneyness of Options

This tells you the relationship between the strike price and the current market price.

### For Call Options (CE):

| Condition | Name | Example (NIFTY at 24,500) |
|---|---|---|
| Strike price < Current price | **In The Money (ITM)** | 24,300 CE, 24,400 CE |
| Strike price = Current price | **At The Money (ATM)** | 24,500 CE |
| Strike price > Current price | **Out of The Money (OTM)** | 24,600 CE, 24,700 CE |

### For Put Options (PE):

| Condition | Name | Example (NIFTY at 24,500) |
|---|---|---|
| Strike price > Current price | **In The Money (ITM)** | 24,600 PE, 24,700 PE |
| Strike price = Current price | **At The Money (ATM)** | 24,500 PE |
| Strike price < Current price | **Out of The Money (OTM)** | 24,300 PE, 24,400 PE |

**Notice that ITM and OTM are REVERSED for calls and puts.**

### Easy Way to Remember

- **Call option:** You want the price to go UP. If the price is already ABOVE your strike, you're ITM (in profit territory).
- **Put option:** You want the price to go DOWN. If the price is already BELOW your strike, you're ITM (in profit territory).

---

### Visual Example

NIFTY spot price: **24,500**

```
CALL OPTIONS (CE)                    PUT OPTIONS (PE)

24,200 CE → Deep ITM                24,200 PE → Deep OTM
24,300 CE → ITM                     24,300 PE → OTM
24,400 CE → Slightly ITM            24,400 PE → Slightly OTM
24,500 CE → ATM                     24,500 PE → ATM
24,600 CE → Slightly OTM            24,600 PE → Slightly ITM
24,700 CE → OTM                     24,700 PE → ITM
24,800 CE → Deep OTM                24,800 PE → Deep ITM
```

---

## Premium Behavior Based on Moneyness

| Type | Premium | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Deep ITM** | Very high premium | Has high intrinsic value |
| **ATM** | Moderate premium | Has maximum time value |
| **Deep OTM** | Very low premium | Very little chance of becoming profitable |

**Beginners are attracted to OTM options because they're cheap.** This is a trap.

OTM options are cheap because they have a very low probability of becoming profitable. Most OTM options expire worthless.

We will cover this in detail in Phase 3. For now, just understand the concept.

# CHAPTER 9: HOW STOCK PRICES ARE DETERMINED

## Supply and Demand

At its core, stock prices are determined by **supply and demand**.

- If more people want to **buy** a stock than sell it → Price goes **UP**
- If more people want to **sell** a stock than buy it → Price goes **DOWN**

### Example

Imagine Infosys is trading at ₹1,500.

**Scenario 1:** Infosys announces better-than-expected quarterly results. Many people now want to buy Infosys shares (demand increases). Sellers realize they can ask for a higher price. The price moves up to ₹1,550.

**Scenario 2:** Infosys loses a major client contract. Many current holders want to sell (supply increases). Buyers know they can offer less. The price drops to ₹1,430.

---

## What Influences Supply and Demand?

| Factor | How It Affects Prices |
|---|---|
| **Company earnings** | Good results → price up, bad results → price down |
| **Economic data** | GDP growth, inflation, interest rates |
| **RBI monetary policy** | Rate cuts → usually positive for markets, rate hikes → usually negative |
| **Global markets** | If US markets fall sharply, Indian markets usually open lower |
| **Industry news** | New regulations, competition, technology changes |
| **Government policy** | Budget announcements, tax changes, infrastructure spending |
| **Investor sentiment** | Fear, greed, panic, euphoria |
| **Foreign Institutional Investors (FII)** | When FIIs buy heavily → market tends to rise, when they sell → market tends to fall |
| **Domestic Institutional Investors (DII)** | Mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds |

---

## The Order Book

At any moment, for any stock on NSE, there is an **order book** showing:

- **Bid:** The highest price a buyer is willing to pay and how many shares they want
- **Ask (Offer):** The lowest price a seller is willing to accept and how many shares they're offering

### Example Order Book for Reliance

| Bid Price | Bid Quantity | Ask Price | Ask Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| ₹2,499 | 500 | ₹2,500 | 300 |
| ₹2,498 | 1,200 | ₹2,501 | 800 |
| ₹2,497 | 750 | ₹2,502 | 450 |
| ₹2,496 | 2,000 | ₹2,503 | 1,100 |
| ₹2,495 | 1,500 | ₹2,504 | 600 |

The **best bid** is ₹2,499. The **best ask** is ₹2,500.

The difference between best bid and best ask (₹2,500 – ₹2,499 = ₹1) is called the **bid-ask spread**.

- If you place a **market buy order**, you'll buy at ₹2,500 (the best ask)
- If you place a **market sell order**, you'll sell at ₹2,499 (the best bid)
- If you place a **limit buy order at ₹2,498**, your order sits in the bid queue and only executes if someone is willing to sell at that price

---

## Last Traded Price (LTP)

The **price you see on your screen** (e.g., "Reliance: ₹2,500") is the **Last Traded Price** — the price at which the most recent trade occurred.

It doesn't mean the next trade will happen at the same price.

# CHAPTER 10: MARKET CAPITALIZATION

## Definition

$$\text{Market Capitalization} = \text{Current Share Price} \times \text{Total Outstanding Shares}$$

## Categories

| Category | Market Cap Range | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Large Cap** | Top 100 companies by market cap (above ~₹60,000 crore) | Reliance, TCS, HDFC Bank, Infosys |
| **Mid Cap** | 101st to 250th company (~₹20,000 to ₹60,000 crore) | Voltas, Trent, Indian Hotels |
| **Small Cap** | 251st onwards (below ~₹20,000 crore) | Many smaller companies |

**NIFTY 50 consists entirely of large-cap companies.**

## Why This Matters

- **Large caps** are more stable, more liquid, and less volatile
- **Small caps** can move 10–20% in a day but carry much higher risk
- **NIFTY options trading** exposes you to large-cap movement, which is generally more predictable than small-cap movement

# CHAPTER 11: MARKET TIMINGS AND SESSIONS

## NSE Trading Hours

| Session | Time | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-open session** | 9:00 AM – 9:08 AM | Orders are collected but not executed. The exchange calculates the **opening price** using a call auction mechanism. |
| **Pre-open order matching** | 9:08 AM – 9:12 AM | Orders are matched and the opening price is determined. |
| **Buffer period** | 9:12 AM – 9:15 AM | Transition to normal market. |
| **Normal market** | 9:15 AM – 3:30 PM | Continuous trading. This is when you will trade. |
| **Closing session** | 3:30 PM – 3:40 PM | Closing price is calculated. |
| **Post-closing session** | 3:40 PM – 4:00 PM | Orders can be placed at the closing price. |

**Key points for you:**

1. **9:15 AM – 9:30 AM:** The first 15 minutes are extremely volatile. Price swings are large and unpredictable. Many experienced traders avoid trading in this window. As a beginner, you should definitely avoid it.

2. **9:30 AM – 2:30 PM:** This is the most suitable window for beginners.

3. **2:30 PM – 3:30 PM:** The last hour can be volatile again. On expiry days (Thursday), the final hour can be especially volatile for options.

4. **Trading days:** Monday to Friday, except stock exchange holidays (published annually by NSE).

# CHAPTER 12: WHAT IS SEBI?

## Full Form

**Securities and Exchange Board of India**

## What Does SEBI Do?

| Function | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Protects investors** | Ensures fair and transparent market practices |
| **Regulates stock exchanges** | Sets rules for NSE, BSE |
| **Regulates brokers** | Ensures brokers follow proper procedures |
| **Prevents fraud** | Investigates insider trading, market manipulation |
| **Regulates mutual funds** | Sets rules for fund management |
| **Monitors FIIs and DIIs** | Tracks institutional activity |
| **Sets margin rules** | Determines how much margin is required for derivatives trading |

## Why SEBI Matters for You

- SEBI decides the margin requirements for NIFTY options
- SEBI regulates lot sizes (and changes them periodically)
- SEBI can change F&O trading rules (they recently increased minimum lot values)
- The SEBI study showing 91–93% of F&O traders lose money is what you should always keep in mind

**Website:** [sebi.gov.in](https://www.sebi.gov.in/)

### Answers

1. A stock/share is a unit of ownership in a company.

2. Primary market is where companies sell shares directly to investors for the first time (IPO). Secondary market is where investors trade shares among themselves on stock exchanges.

3. BSE and NSE. NSE is more important for F&O trading (99%+ of derivatives volume).

4. NIFTY 50 is an index of 50 large, liquid companies listed on NSE, weighted by free-float market capitalization. It contains 50 stocks.

5. Yes, significantly. HDFC Bank has ~13% weight. A 3% drop in HDFC Bank would pull NIFTY down by approximately 0.39% (0.13 × 3%), which at NIFTY 24,500 would be about 95 points. This is a rough approximation.

6. A call option gives the right to buy at the strike price. A put option gives the right to sell at the strike price. You buy calls when you expect the price to rise and puts when you expect it to fall.

7. OTM (Out of The Money) — because the strike price (24,700) is above the current price (24,500) for a call option.

8. OTM (Out of The Money) — because the strike price (24,300) is below the current price (24,500) for a put option. For puts, ITM means strike > current price.

9. The premium paid. That is the maximum loss for an option buyer.

10. The price paid (by buyer) or received (by seller) for an options contract.

11. Check [nseindia.com](https://www.nseindia.com/) for the current lot size. It has historically been 50 or 75, and SEBI revises this.

12. Normal market hours: 9:15 AM to 3:30 PM. Pre-open session starts at 9:00 AM.

13. Securities and Exchange Board of India. It regulates the stock market, protects investors, prevents fraud, and sets rules for exchanges, brokers, and derivatives.

14. Not necessarily. Trading charges (brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST, stamp duty) can consume a significant portion of the ₹5 profit. For small moves, you might have zero or negative net profit after costs.

15. The difference between the best (highest) bid price and the best (lowest) ask price. It represents the cost of immediate execution.

16. Free-float market capitalization considers only the shares available for public trading (excludes promoter-held and locked-in shares). It's used to calculate index weights.

17. HDFC Bank (~13%), Reliance Industries (~10%), ICICI Bank (~8%). Approximate values — check current weights for exact numbers.

18. Bank NIFTY is an index of 12 large banking stocks on NSE. NIFTY 50 covers 50 stocks across multiple sectors. Bank NIFTY is more concentrated (one sector) and more volatile.

19. 91–93% of individual F&O traders lose money (SEBI studies from 2024–2025).

20. In a futures contract, both buyer and seller are **obligated** to settle the trade. In an options contract, the buyer has the **right** but not the obligation — the seller has the obligation.
